LC 42, trapping rain water: four solutions to one question

2026-09-28 · 18 min · Stack Frame

The problem

Given bars of unit width, compute how much rain water they trap.

[0,1,0,2,1,0,1,3,2,1,2,1]  ->  6
[4,2,0,3,2,5]              ->  9

These two answers are easy to mix up, so pin them down now; they serve as anchors for everything below.

One sentence

Every solution starts by reducing the problem to a single position: the water above a column depends only on the tallest bar to its left and the tallest to its right, and the limiting side is the shorter one, exactly the leaky-bucket principle.

water[i] = max(0, min(leftMax, rightMax) - height[i])

Check it on the first example at the fifth index, which has height zero: two bars to the left, three to the right, so the shorter bound is two and the cell holds two units, the deepest single cell in the figure.

So the whole problem asks one thing: what are the left and right maxima at each position? The four methods are four ways of obtaining those two numbers.

def trap(height):
    n = len(height)
    leftMax = [0] * n
    rightMax = [0] * n

    leftMax[0] = height[0]
    for i in range(1, n):
        leftMax[i] = max(leftMax[i - 1], height[i])

    rightMax[n - 1] = height[n - 1]
    for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
        rightMax[i] = max(rightMax[i + 1], height[i])

    return sum(min(leftMax[i], rightMax[i]) - height[i] for i in range(n))

That is linear time with two arrays. The next question is whether the arrays can be avoided.

def trap(height):
    l, r = 0, len(height) - 1
    left_max = right_max = 0
    ans = 0

    while l < r:
        if height[l] < height[r]:
            left_max = max(left_max, height[l])
            ans += left_max - height[l]
            l += 1
        else:
            right_max = max(right_max, height[r])
            ans += right_max - height[r]
            r -= 1

    return ans

Note the order inside each branch: update the running maximum first, then settle. That way a new record automatically contributes zero, since the running maximum becomes the bar itself, and no conditional is needed. Settling before updating would produce a negative contribution and subtract from the total.

The first two cells contribute nothing because each is a new record and therefore its own boundary. Three consecutive right settlements occur while the left side stands on a bar of height two, and the running right maximum stops changing once the tallest bar on that side is passed. The turning point is where the left maximum catches up, after which every remaining settlement is on the left and the deep well in the middle is filled in one sweep. The largest single contribution is the deepest cell, two units.

Why the shorter end can be settled immediately

This is the heart of the matter, and the part most memorisers cannot explain. The running maximum is only a local bound so far, not a global one, and the right side is still unexplored.

First step: if the left bar is shorter, then the right side contains at least one bar of height at least the right cursor's, which is taller than the left bar. So the true right maximum strictly exceeds the left bar, meaning water cannot drain to the right.

That alone is not enough, since the shorter side could still be the left. The second step is a contradiction. Suppose the running left maximum exceeded the true right maximum. Then it came from some tall bar already passed. At the moment the left pointer crossed that bar, the left-shorter test failed there, so the algorithm turned and settled on the right, contracting the right side until either a taller bar appeared or the pointers met. Either way, by the time the pointer returns to settle the current cell, the true right maximum cannot be below the running left maximum.

So for the shorter end, the outer bound is already guaranteed by the opposite bar, and the opposite side cannot be the limiting one. The water level can therefore be computed from the known side alone, which is why no preprocessing is required.

One line: settle the shorter side because its answer is already determined unilaterally.

The other axis: a monotonic stack

The first three methods cut vertically: stand on a column and compute its whole water column at once. The stack cuts horizontally: find a basin and settle a whole layer.

def trap(height):
    stack = []
    ans = 0

    for i in range(len(height)):
        while stack and height[i] > height[stack[-1]]:
            top = stack.pop()
            if not stack:
                break
            width = i - stack[-1] - 1
            bounded = min(height[i], height[stack[-1]]) - height[top]
            ans += width * bounded
        stack.append(i)

    return ans

The stack holds indices whose right boundary has not yet appeared, in decreasing height order. When a taller bar arrives, the top is the basin floor, the next entry down is the left wall and the current bar is the right wall. The width is the gap between the walls, and the height is the shorter wall minus the floor, the same bucket principle. The pop must loop, because one tall bar can close several layers at once.

The clearest evidence of the horizontal view: the water above one particular column arrives as two separate additions in the stack method and as one lump sum in the pointer method. Same final answer, different bookkeeping, which is the best footnote to why the stack works: it does not compute how much a column holds, it computes how wide a layer spreads.

Both linear time, differing only in space.

Three dimensions: when left and right become everywhere

The two-dimensional version gives a height matrix where each cell can leak in four directions.

The formula does not transfer directly, since a cell's surroundings are not two directions but every path to the boundary. But translating it does the work: the left maximum is the bottleneck of the path from the cell to the left boundary, and the right maximum is the bottleneck of the other path, so the water level is the smaller of two path bottlenecks, and in two dimensions it is the minimum over all paths.

That is the minimum-bottleneck path problem, a standard Dijkstra variant that minimises the maximum edge instead of the sum.

import heapq

def trapRainWater(heightMap):
    m, n = len(heightMap), len(heightMap[0])
    if m < 3 or n < 3:
        return 0

    visited = [[False] * n for _ in range(m)]
    heap = []
    for i in range(m):
        for j in range(n):
            if i == 0 or i == m - 1 or j == 0 or j == n - 1:
                visited[i][j] = True
                heapq.heappush(heap, (heightMap[i][j], i, j))

    ans = 0
    while heap:
        h, x, y = heapq.heappop(heap)
        for dx, dy in ((1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)):
            nx, ny = x + dx, y + dy
            if 0 <= nx < m and 0 <= ny < n and not visited[nx][ny]:
                visited[nx][ny] = True
                if heightMap[nx][ny] < h:
                    ans += h - heightMap[nx][ny]
                heapq.heappush(heap, (max(heightMap[nx][ny], h), nx, ny))

    return ans

Three points. Taking the lowest point on the current wall is correct by the same argument as the one-dimensional pointers: when processing the lowest wall height, every unvisited cell must cross the wall on any path to the boundary, so its water level is at least that height, and the heap guarantees it is exactly that. That is Dijkstra's greedy argument verbatim.

Pushing the maximum of the cell height and the current water level matters because a flooded cell is no longer a bar of its own height for cells further in; it becomes a wall of the water level. This line is the easiest place to go wrong.

Marking on push rather than on pop works because the first push carries the final level, since later paths can only be higher, which is the same reason the pointer version updates before settling.

Order is mn log mn with linear space.

The one-dimensional version is a degenerate case of the two-dimensional one, with only two directions of paths, and the two-dimensional method can also solve the one-dimensional problem.

Java

public int trap(int[] height) {
    int l = 0, r = height.length - 1;
    int leftMax = 0, rightMax = 0, ans = 0;

    while (l < r) {
        if (height[l] < height[r]) {
            leftMax = Math.max(leftMax, height[l]);
            ans += leftMax - height[l];
            l++;
        } else {
            rightMax = Math.max(rightMax, height[r]);
            ans += rightMax - height[r];
            r--;
        }
    }
    return ans;
}

The three-dimensional version uses a priority queue with an explicit comparator, since the Java default is a max-heap.

  1. Settling before updating the running maximum, which produces negative contributions at new records.
  2. Reversing the comparison, so the taller end gets settled while its outer bound is still unknown, inflating the answer. The official sample cannot detect this because the two ends differ by exactly one and both variants give six. Test with a case where the bug shows.
  3. Popping the stack without checking emptiness, which reads out of range when there is no left wall.
  4. Storing values in the stack instead of indices, which makes the width formula impossible.
  5. Forgetting the maximum in the three-dimensional push, which lets a flooded cell act as dry ground and understates interior levels.
  6. Omitting the small-dimension guard, which happens to return zero rather than by design.

How to answer it

Give the formula first as the bucket principle, then brute force and the prefix arrays as the ladder. Then the pointers with the proof, which is the discriminator: the shorter side has its outer bound guaranteed by the opposite bar, and the contradiction shows the opposite side cannot be limiting. Then the horizontal viewpoint and the observation that a single column's water arrives in several instalments. Then the three-dimensional generalisation through path bottlenecks into Dijkstra.

assert trap([0,1,0,2,1,0,1,3,2,1,2,1]) == 6
assert trap([4,2,0,3,2,5]) == 9
assert trap([5,0,1]) == 1
assert trap([4,0,0,0,3]) == 9
assert trap([2,0,2]) == 2

Closing

What makes this problem attractive is that one question wears several faces. Brute force and prefix arrays say compute both bounds everywhere. The pointers say compute only the half already determined. The stack says stop cutting vertically and fill by layers, so the same cell may be paid in instalments. And the three-dimensional version says that left and right were always special cases of a path bottleneck, at which point trapping rain water is a shortest-path problem.

Behind all four is one principle: water rests on the shorter side. In one dimension that is the shorter of two bars; in two dimensions, the gentlest of all paths to the boundary.
