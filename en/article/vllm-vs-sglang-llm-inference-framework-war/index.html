From vLLM to SGLang: The Ultimate Arms Race in LLM Inference Frameworks

As competition among training frameworks gradually converges, the fight is shifting wholesale to the inference side. A single paper, a single PR, a single commit can double a framework's throughput overnight — that is business as usual in the 2025–2026 LLM inference ecosystem. This article puts vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, TGI, and LMDeploy on the same table to see what each one is betting on, and what exactly they are all racing to optimize.

Introduction: Why Inference Frameworks Became Contested Ground

For the past three years, the story of large models was one of "training": whoever had more parameters, more data, more GPUs won. But once Scaling Laws pushed training costs into the hundreds of millions of dollars, the industry's attention began to shift to another battlefield — inference. Three changes turned inference frameworks into a core arena overnight:
  1. Models grew enormous: GPT-4o, DeepSeek V4, Kimi K2, and GLM-5.2 routinely carry hundreds of billions of MoE parameters, and the compute cost per token scales linearly with the number of experts;
  2. Call volume exploded: the agent paradigm turns one "conversation" into dozens or hundreds of round trips, pushing inference QPS orders of magnitude above early ChatGPT;
  3. End-to-end costs inverted: training is a one-time investment, inference is a recurring expense — by Gartner's reckoning, 70%–80% of the compute over a large model's lifetime goes to inference.
In one sentence: training decides whether you get to play; inference decides how long you survive. That is why the company behind vLLM went from a blank whiteboard to a team of dozens, why SGLang raised hundreds of millions of dollars in under two years, and why Nvidia rewrites the kernels for every generation of TensorRT-LLM.

1. What Are Inference Frameworks Actually Competing On?

Strip away the slogans and an inference framework really has to answer three questions:
Dimension Plain-English explanation Why it is hard
Throughput How many tokens one machine can emit per second Directly determines per-GPU ROI
Latency How long a user waits from pressing Enter to seeing the first character Directly determines the product experience
Memory How many requests a single H100 can serve at once VRAM is a physical gift from HBM — every inch you save counts
Throughput and latency are inherently in tension: bigger batches raise throughput, but every request must wait for the batch to fill, so latency climbs. Every framework's "engineering arms race" is really a search for a new Pareto frontier inside that three-way trade-off — making possible the compromise points others considered impossible. Here is how each of the five plays its hand.

2. vLLM: The First Cut, Opened by PagedAttention

Before vLLM, KV cache waste was the elephant in the room for serving frameworks. The conventional approach pre-allocated a contiguous slab of VRAM per request, sized for the longest possible sequence. Real request lengths vary wildly — 10% of long requests eat 80% of the reserved memory, while short requests leave holes behind. The result: memory fragmentation and utilization below 40%, so the concurrency a single 80GB H100 could sustain was capped by memory before anything else. vLLM's contribution was to move the operating system's paged virtual memory onto the GPU:
  • KV cache is chopped into fixed-size blocks (typically 16 tokens each);
  • a logical sequence uses a block table to point at physical blocks, which can be scattered, shared, and allocated on demand;
  • just as with OS pages, logically adjacent does not mean physically adjacent, so fragmentation dissolves on its own.
Paired with a rewritten PagedAttention kernel that lets attention read K/V straight out of scattered blocks — that was the hardest engineering cut of all.
In its 2023 paper, vLLM reported 2–24× higher throughput than HuggingFace Transformers. The keyword: "Continuous Batching" — dynamically packing new requests into a running batch so the GPU does not idle just because one request finished early.
Where vLLM stands: general-purpose, easy to use, thickest ecosystem. Nearly every open-source model gets a vLLM adaptation on release day. The trade-off: in narrow niches such as structured generation, long context, and disaggregated batching, it is not the fastest one.

3. SGLang: RadixAttention and a New Paradigm for Structured Generation

SGLang was released in 2024 by the LMSYS team (the same people behind Vicuna and Chatbot Arena), and it targeted not "general inference" but an overlooked scenario — structured output. Honestly: getting an LLM to emit valid JSON, a SQL statement, or a tool-call schema is something traditional frameworks do badly. Three reasons:
  1. Constrained decoding has to mask out illegal tokens at every step, and that is not cheap;
  2. Shared prefixes go unreused — ten requests all start with "You are a translation assistant," yet the framework computes that same prefix ten times;
  3. Branching generation is inherently wasteful — asking for several candidate answers or several tool calls spins up several independent KV caches.
SGLang's move was to organize the KV cache as a radix tree:
  • each edge of the tree corresponds to a span of tokens, and each node stores the KV for that span;
  • multiple requests that share a prefix automatically reuse the same subtree — prefix caching got native support at the data-structure level for the first time;
  • the multi-candidate branches of structured generation ("give me 3 different translations") hang directly off the tree, branch nodes share the root, and redundant computation disappears.
Combined with the Zero-Overhead Scheduler and a compressed finite state machine (FSM), SGLang reports being 3–7× faster than vLLM on JSON, SQL, and function-calling workloads, with a 5× average end-to-end improvement.
SGLang's real killer feature is not raw single-point speed; it is that the idea "generation is structured and reusable" was written into the underlying abstraction. Once your application is an agent, a workflow, or a multi-turn tool caller, SGLang's advantage compounds.
Where SGLang stands: long context, structured output, shared prefixes, branching generation — across every niche where vLLM is weak, SGLang is redefining the baseline.

4. Head-to-Head: Choosing Among Five Mainstream Frameworks

Here are the five frameworks everyone names, side by side:
Framework Camp Core technology Strongest scenario Weak spot
vLLM UC Berkeley / independent, VC-backed PagedAttention + Continuous Batching General open-source models, easy deployment, thickest ecosystem Not the strongest for structured generation or long context
SGLang LMSYS / commercialized RadixAttention + compressed FSM + zero-overhead scheduling Structured output, shared prefixes, agent workflows Started later on general-purpose serving; smaller community than vLLM
TensorRT-LLM Nvidia Operator fusion + in-flight batching + INT8/FP8 Nvidia hardware, minimal latency, enterprise RAG Locked to Nvidia cards, hard to modify kernels, unusable on non-NV hardware
TGI Hugging Face Continuous Batching + quantization HF ecosystem, Dockerized deployment, European market Performance often trails vLLM by half a release; slow iteration
LMDeploy OpenMMLab / Shanghai AI Lab TurboMind kernels + KV quantization Domestic-hardware support, quantization, the Chinese community Thin international ecosystem; narrower model coverage than vLLM
Three one-liners: - Want the fastest path to a working deployment → vLLM - Writing lots of agents / tool calling → SGLang - Must squeeze latency to single-digit ms and only run NV cards → TensorRT-LLM
Below, we take apart the four focal technologies of this arms race, because those four are exactly what these five are competing over.

5. Focal Point One: KV Cache Management

KV cache is the biggest line item in inference memory — the longer the sequence, the more it takes; the larger the batch, the more it takes.
Approach Exemplar Idea Cost
PagedAttention vLLM OS-style paging plus a block table; scattered allocation eliminates fragmentation Requires rewriting the kernel
RadixAttention SGLang KV organized as a radix tree; prefixes reused by construction More complex write path
PagedAttention-MoE / cache pooling Nvidia / industry practice Reuse KV across requests and across experts Requires hardware coherence support
The industry trend worth calling out separately is Prefix Cache — persisting the KV of system prompts and long document prefixes in VRAM or host memory and reusing it across requests. vLLM added --enable-prefix-caching in late 2024; SGLang built it into its data structures from day 1. In RAG, tool-calling, and long-system-prompt scenarios, prefix caching can cut time-to-first-token by more than 60%, which makes it one of the hottest optimization directions today.

6. Focal Point Two: Continuous Batching and Schedulers

Traditional batching is static — wait for N requests, start, run to completion, then admit the next batch. The GPU burns a lot of time waiting for batches. Continuous Batching lets the in-flight batch change dynamically:
  • a request that finishes generating exits immediately and frees its memory;
  • a new request fills the free slot right away, so the GPU almost never idles;
  • with iteration-level scheduling, prefill (the first pass that computes KV) and decode (token-by-token generation) can even be mixed into the same batch.
Traditional batching:
  [A,B,C] ──────────── wait until full ────────────► [done]   [D,E] ───►
       ^ the long-tail request C drags the whole batch down, leaving lots of GPU idle time

Continuous batching:
  [A,B,C      ] ─► [A,B,C,D] ─► [  B,D,E,F] ─► [     D,E,F]
       A finishes early → D takes the slot at once → E,F flow in → GPU utilization nears the ceiling
The next level of this race is disaggregated prefill/decode — splitting prefill and decode onto different GPUs or clusters:
  • prefill is compute-bound and suits cards built for raw math;
  • decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and suits high-bandwidth cards;
  • once separated, each class of card does what it does best, and overall throughput takes another step up. vLLM, SGLang, and TRT-LLM all followed this path in 2025–2026.

7. Focal Point Three: Speculative Decoding and Speculative Sampling

Autoregressive LLMs run the entire model forward once per token, burning compute on guessing a single character. Speculative decoding works like this:
  1. a small draft model quickly "guesses" K tokens;
  2. the large model verifies all K tokens in parallel in a single forward pass (one pass = K candidates);
  3. correct guesses are accepted outright; wrong ones are rolled back and recomputed.
Payoff: 2–3× end-to-end speedup when the draft and target distributions are close enough.
Variant Idea Representative implementations
Vanilla speculative decoding Small model guesses, large model verifies Already supported by vLLM, TGI, and TRT-LLM
N-gram / Prompt Lookup No draft model; look up candidate n-grams directly in the prompt vLLM --speculative-model "[ngram]"
EAGLE-2 / EAGLE-3 Drafting at the feature-vector level, pushing acceptance rates above 60% Integrated in the SGLang and vLLM communities
Medusa Add multiple heads that guess several tokens in parallel TRT-LLM
Speculative decoding is currently the best value-for-money acceleration technique out there: no touching model weights, no retraining, just a few lines of scheduling on the inference side, and throughput can double. That is why virtually every mainstream framework put "supports some form of speculative decoding" on the first line of its release notes in 2025.

8. Focal Point Four: Quantization

Quantization is fundamentally about representing the same weights and activations with fewer bits, squeezing memory down to the last drop.
Quantization scheme Bit width Calibration required Accuracy loss Best supported by
GPTQ / AWQ INT4/INT8 Yes Moderate vLLM, LMDeploy, TGI
SmoothQuant INT8 Yes Small TRT-LLM, LMDeploy
FP8 (post-H100) E4M3 / E5M2 No Minimal TRT-LLM, vLLM 0.6+
INT4 GEMM (W4A16) INT4 weights / FP16 activations Yes Moderate TRT-LLM, LMDeploy TurboMind
Worth emphasizing on its own: FP8.
  • H100 / H200 natively support FP8 Tensor Cores, doubling compute density versus FP16;
  • it needs almost no calibration, and the accuracy loss sits inside benchmark noise;
  • since 2025 it has become the "infrastructure-grade" default — one flag in vLLM 0.6+, the out-of-the-box setting in TRT-LLM.
In short: quantization before FP8 was "saves memory but is not necessarily faster"; after FP8 it is "both fast and cheap". It is the gift Nvidia's hardware handed to the inference ecosystem.

9. Selection Advice: Which Card to Play in Which Scenario

Your scenario Recommended framework Why
General chat / compatibility with all HuggingFace models vLLM Thickest ecosystem, broadest model coverage, fastest community response
Agents / tool calling / heavy JSON output SGLang RadixAttention reuses prefixes; structured generation is 3–7× faster
Long-context RAG with shared system prompts SGLang, or vLLM + Prefix Cache KV reuse halves time-to-first-token outright
All-Nvidia cluster, enterprise SLA, single-digit ms required TensorRT-LLM Operator fusion + FP8 define the latency floor
Domestic accelerators (Huawei / MetaX / Biren / Enflame) LMDeploy / vLLM LMDeploy has the most complete domestic support; vLLM has the broader open ecosystem
Multimodal / LLaVA / Qwen-VL vLLM Fastest multimodal branches and model onboarding
A rule of thumb: get it running on vLLM first, then ask where the business bottleneck actually is. - Bottleneck is "long context / repeated system prompts" → add Prefix Cache or move to SGLang; - Bottleneck is "latency will not go any lower" → speculative decoding + FP8, and TRT-LLM if that is still not enough; - Bottleneck is "memory" → quantization + PagedAttention, plus prefill/decode disaggregation when necessary.

10. Where the Ultimate Arms Race Leads: From Framework to Infrastructure

Stack all of this competition together and one common trend appears — inference frameworks are shedding their "framework" shell and becoming a class of infrastructure:
  1. From single node to cluster: yesterday's inference framework managed a few cards in one box; today it has to handle cross-machine request routing, KV migration, prefill/decode separation, and expert routing and scheduling;
  2. From general to scenario-specific: vLLM is no longer "any model, any scenario," and SGLang is no longer just "structured generation" — each framework is growing its own domain language (vLLM's LLMEngine, SGLang's @function, TRT-LLM's builder DSL);
  3. From software to software-hardware co-design: FP8, CPO, IBGDA, NVLink C2C — every step in hardware rewrites the optimization space available to software. When RDMA halves cross-node latency, the engineering payoff of prefill/decode disaggregation climbs another notch;
  4. From inference to "inference + training": DeepEP, NCCL, and SGLang's explorations on the training side — the people whose frameworks understand both inference and training will be the ones holding scheduling in their hands.
So the essence of this arms race is not who is faster, but who becomes the default choice of infrastructure for the large-model era. vLLM is betting on "general-purpose dominance," SGLang on "structure and agents," TRT-LLM on "the full Nvidia stack," and LMDeploy on "domestic hardware and quantization."

Closing Notes

The ultimate arms race in inference frameworks is not about squeezing another 10% of throughput; it is about making "one card's worth of GPUs do the work of two" into a gift beyond what physics already gave us.
Back to the three questions from the beginning — throughput, latency, memory — three years of engineering practice has produced these answers:
  • once PagedAttention swept away memory fragmentation, the concurrency a single machine could hold made its first order-of-magnitude leap;
  • once Continuous Batching stopped the GPU from idling, the throughput baseline was drawn a second time;
  • once RadixAttention wrote prefixes into the data structure, structured generation went from "barely workable" to "natively efficient";
  • once FP8, speculative decoding, and prefill/decode disaggregation stacked on top of each other, the marginal cost per token fell to a level unimaginable three years ago;
  • once ecosystem co-building pulled training, communication, inference, and scheduling into a single drawer, inference frameworks became eligible for infrastructure status for the first time.
None of those five on its own rewrote history, but stacked together they have already made deploying a hundred-billion-parameter MoE model in 2026 cheaper than deploying a 7B model was in 2023. That is the most valuable legacy this arms race leaves the industry. Over the next three years, the inference framework story will shift from "who is faster" to "who is the default." As for who gets that ticket — vLLM, SGLang, or some dark horse that has not surfaced yet — that is not a question one technical article can answer. But one thing is certain: single-point optimization is no longer enough; whoever can stake throughput, latency, memory, and ecosystem on the same card is the one with a chance to stay at the table.
