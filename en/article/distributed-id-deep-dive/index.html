Distributed IDs, measured: page splits, a thousand clock rollbacks, and segments

2026-11-04 · 16 min · Stack Frame

Who this is for: backend engineers whose systems moved from one machine to several and who now have to decide what the primary key should be.

Measurements use plain Python and the standard library; the reasoning applies to any stack and any database.

Reading time around 16 minutes. Level: intermediate.

Two data centres, each generating its own IDs

The day the system was split horizontally, two instances of the order service landed in different data centres. Using the database's auto-increment seemed obvious, and both instances connected to the database in their own data centre.

Both started issuing from one. Replication collided on the primary key and the sync job stayed red all night.

Someone proposed UUIDs and was told they are unordered, so primary key inserts into the storage engine collapse. Someone proposed Snowflake and was asked what happens when the machine clock goes backwards. Three proposals, three objections, and nobody could say whether the objections were real.

So this post implements all four mainstream approaches, and measures the two contested points: how much the unordered write pattern actually costs, and what a clock rollback does without protection.

First, decide what the ID is for

RequirementHard or softNote
Globally uniqueHardNever repeats across machines or data centres
Roughly increasingSoftFriendly to a B+ tree, and it makes ordering by ID equal ordering by time
Generated without the networkSoftWhether a central issuer going down takes the business with it
No business meaningSoftDoes not leak that there were only three orders today
Short enoughSoftSixty-four bits beats a thirty-six character string in index size, memory and joins

No scheme scores full marks. Auto-increment is ordered but central, UUID is unordered, Snowflake is ordered but fears the clock, segments compromise and cost an extra prefetch. Engineering choice means finding the one hard requirement and picking the scheme that does not break it.

Database auto-increment: good until it has to be distributed

On one database it is perfect: ordered, compact, no cognitive load. It dies in two places once the system spreads. Issuing capacity equals database write capacity, so the database becomes the bottleneck at peak. And multiple databases necessarily collide, since two sequences know nothing about each other.

The usual patch, giving each shard a stride so they interleave, works for a while and has two traps. Expanding from two shards to three means re-planning strides and splitting historical ranges, which is miserable. And the ranges are inherently unordered, so the ID cannot serve as a proxy for time.

Its place is clear: single database, moderate write concurrency, and no plan to shard. If sharding is foreseeable, do not start with it.

UUID: globally unique and a poor match for a B+ tree

A version four UUID is fully random, 128 bits, with a collision probability that is effectively zero in engineering terms. It solves the multi-data-centre problem by removing the need to coordinate at all.

The price is in the database. A clustered index stores rows physically in primary key order, and a random key distributes insert points across the whole tree: page splits with data movement, a working set far larger than the hot pages so the buffer pool hit rate falls, and a wider key, since thirty-six characters or sixteen bytes against eight makes every secondary index leaf bigger.

That last effect is multiplicative and often forgotten. Five secondary indexes each store a copy of the key, so a key four times wider makes five indexes four times wider. Which is why a random UUID as a primary key and the same UUID as an ordinary unique column have completely different performance profiles.

If a UUID is required, generate an ordered one. Version seven puts a timestamp in the front and became an RFC standard in 2024, which restores roughly increasing order and largely fixes the split problem. New systems should prefer version seven over version four.

Segment allocation: one round trip for ten thousand IDs

UPDATE alloc SET max_id = max_id + step, updated_at = NOW()
    WHERE biz_tag = 'order';
-- read the new max_id back and cache the range in memory

Instead of asking the database for each ID, ask for a batch and hand it out locally. Issuing becomes a pure memory operation, and database pressure drops to one round trip per ten thousand IDs. At a hundred thousand issuances per second with a step of ten thousand, the database sees ten updates per second.

The necessary engineering detail is a double buffer. With one buffer, the moment it runs out is the moment you fetch, and requests stall on the database, which shows up as latency spikes. With two, the next range is prefetched when the current one is ten or twenty percent consumed, and the switch is invisible.

Its weaknesses are plain. IDs are not strictly increasing, only within a range, so two instances holding different ranges can issue a smaller number after a larger one. A restart wastes the unused part of a range, which can be mitigated by persisting progress past ninety percent consumption. And it needs an allocation table plus a service to run.

It fits internal identifiers that need no strict ordering but want compact, high-throughput values.

Snowflake: the art of bit allocation

| 1 reserved | 41 timestamp | 10 machine | 12 sequence |
  • Forty-one bits of milliseconds is about sixty-nine years from a custom epoch.
  • Ten bits of machine identity is 1024 instances, often split into five bits of data centre and five of machine.
  • Twelve bits of sequence allows 4096 IDs per millisecond per machine, so 4.096 million per second on one instance.

The strength is decisive: pure in-memory bit arithmetic, roughly increasing, no central dependency. The fatal flaw is in those forty-one bits: the timestamp comes from the local clock, and the local clock is not trustworthy. NTP corrections move it backwards, virtual machine migrations drift it, and people have changed it by hand. Once it goes backwards, a new ID can collide with one already issued.

Measurement one: rollbacks without protection

A simulator drives a virtual clock with a fixed seed, generating a hundred thousand IDs while injecting two hundred NTP-style corrections, each moving the clock back fifty milliseconds.

ProtectionCollisionsExplanation
None, the standard implementation10,000Each rollback replays fifty already-used milliseconds, and two hundred times fifty is ten thousand
Spin until the clock catches up0Refuses to issue while the timestamp is not greater than the last one

The unprotected number matches the arithmetic exactly: as many milliseconds as you roll back, that many IDs collide. And the collisions cluster in the fifty milliseconds after each correction, which is precisely the window that already issued orders. In business terms, one rollback collides every order created in that window.

The guarded version has costs worth seeing. Issuing pauses for the length of the rollback, so fifty milliseconds of rollback means up to fifty milliseconds of waiting, which is usually fine. And it quietly spends the future: to keep timestamps increasing, the sequence runs ahead of the real clock and does not recover on its own. A large rollback, tens of minutes say, should raise an alarm and refuse rather than spin.

So a production strategy has three levels: spin for small rollbacks under a hundred milliseconds; refuse and alert for large ones, because a visible error beats a silent duplicate; and as a backstop, reserve a few bits as a rollback generation, incrementing it each time the clock goes back so the already-used interval is skipped.

Measurement two: throughput

SchemeMeasuredCost per ID
Version four UUID1.16 million per secondOne random generation
Snowflake3.31 million per secondA few shifts and additions
SegmentsLocal issuing is unbounded; the limit is prefetchingTwenty round trips for two hundred thousand IDs

Snowflake is three times faster than a random UUID even in Python, and in a compiled language the gap is larger because the random generation dominates. Ordered and faster is the case for avoiding random UUIDs as keys.

For segments, the number to read is the round trip count: two hundred thousand IDs for twenty round trips, so at a millisecond each the ceiling is ten million per second, set by the step you choose. The cost is memory and the wasted tail of a range on restart.

Below about four hundred thousand per second on a single instance, all three are over-provisioned. The bottleneck is usually elsewhere, so do not pay for throughput you cannot use; pay for the constraints that actually bind.

A decision tree and a checklist

  1. Is sharding foreseeable? If not, auto-increment and stop.
  2. Is the ID shown to users, as an order or payment number? Then do not use a sequential value, because it leaks volume and invites enumeration. Use segments or Snowflake plus a business prefix and a check digit, or design a separate business number.
  3. Internal key, needs ordering, no extra component? Snowflake with the three-level clock protection, or version seven UUID.
  4. Needs to be short, explainable and rate-controlled, and one more service is acceptable? Segments.
  • Machine identity assignment is explicit, whether static, allocated by the platform or registered in a lease. Duplicate machine IDs turn Snowflake into a collision generator.
  • The three levels of clock protection are all present, with a threshold and an alert.
  • The custom epoch is a documented constant shared by the whole team.
  • Tables with random UUID keys have their secondary index growth and split behaviour evaluated; new tables prefer version seven.
  • Segments use a double buffer and a step sized for ten minutes of peak.
  • Externally visible IDs are non-sequential and non-enumerable, or the exposure is deliberate and accepted.
  • The availability of the issuing path matches the business: Snowflake needs nothing central, and segment services need a local fallback when they fail.

Closing

The two-data-centre incident ended with Snowflake as the primary key, each data centre issuing independently, and a separate short business number generated from segments with a check digit. Three years in production, no collisions, and the clock protection raised two alerts, both handled invisibly.

The lesson is not to pick the most advanced algorithm. It is to identify the one hard constraint and choose the simplest scheme that satisfies it. Most systems land somewhere plain: no sharding means auto-increment, sharding with full control means Snowflake, and everything else can live with segments.
